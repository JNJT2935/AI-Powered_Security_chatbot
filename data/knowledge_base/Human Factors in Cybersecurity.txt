Identification is the process of a subject claiming, or professing, an identity.

A subject must provide an identity to a system to start the authentication, authorization, and accounting processes.

A core identification principle is that all subjects must have unique identities.

Ways of proving identity include typing a username, swiping a smartcard, speaking a phrase, or positioning your face, hand, or finger in front of a camera.

Authentication verifies the subject's identity by comparing one or more factors against a database of valid identities.

The authentication information used to verify identity is private and needs to be protected.

Passwords are rarely stored in cleartext within a database, instead authentication systems store hashes of passwords.

There are three common factors used for authentication: something you know, something you have, and something you are.

Something you know includes a password or pin.

Something you have includes a smartcard or mobile device.

Something you are includes a fingerprint or other biometric method.

Somewhere You Are identifies a subject's location based on a specific computer, device, geographic location, or phone number.

Many mobile device management systems use context-aware authentication to identify mobile device users.

Identification and authentication occur together as a single two-step process.

Providing an identity is the first step, and providing the authentication information is the second step.

Without both identification and authentication, a subject must not gain access to a system.

A subject is an active entity that accesses a passive object to receive information from, or data about, an object.

Subjects can be users, programs, processes, services, computers, or anything else that can access a resource.

An object is a passive entity that provides information to active subjects.

Examples of objects include files, databases, computers, programs, processes, services, printers, and storage media.

The roles of subject and object can switch back and forth when two entities interact.

The subject is always the active entity that receives information about, or data from, the object.

The object is always the passive entity that provides or hosts the information or data.

Authorization indicates who is trusted to perform specific operations.

If the action is allowed, the subject is authorized; if disallowed, the subject is not authorized.

Subjects are authorized to access specific objects based on their proven identity.

If a user attempts to open a file, the authorization mechanism checks to ensure that the user has at least read permission on the file.

Administrators grant users only the privileges they need to perform their jobs following the principle of least privilege.

Password entropy refers to how unpredictable your password or phrase is.

Password entropy measures password randomness and unpredictability, measured in bits.

The more bits of entropy there are, the more complex a password is and the tougher it is to break through brute force attacks.

Types of user accounts vary from system to system and from organization to organization.

The common types of user accounts are standard user accounts, service user accounts, privileged user accounts, and guest user accounts.

A standard user account has the right to do most everyday tasks on a system.

A standard user account cannot do advanced tasks like installing applications or making significant changes to system settings.

Standard user accounts are used for personal use, work environments, and the education sector.

Standard user accounts are common targets for phishing attacks where attackers trick users into revealing their credentials.

Users may choose weak or easily guessable passwords making accounts vulnerable to brute force attacks.

Recommendations for standard user accounts include educating users on phishing and implementing multi-factor authentication.

A service account is a special type of account used by applications or services to interact with other applications, services, or the operating system.

Service accounts are not associated with a specific person but provide a security context for services.

Examples of local service accounts built into Windows computers include LocalService, LocalSystem, and NetworkService.

Service accounts support business-critical programs and processes such as databases.

Service accounts are used to run scripts and automated tasks that interact with sensitive data.

Service accounts have extensive access to system resources making them prime targets for hackers.

Service accounts sometimes use static passwords that are not frequently updated.

Misuse of service accounts can lead to privilege escalation, spoofing, nonrepudiation, and information disclosure.

Recommendations for service accounts include creating unique accounts for each service and limiting permissions to the minimum necessary.

A guest user account is a limited account that does not require a password.

Guest user accounts are ideal for users who need temporary access without compromising system security.

Usually all user data is erased once the guest logs out.

Guest user accounts are used for visitors, shared computers, and temporary access for testing.

Guest accounts should not access sensitive data or perform administrative tasks.

Long or unlimited guest sessions can be exploited if a guest leaves the session active.

Guest accounts should be disabled when not needed to reduce the risk of unauthorized access.

A privileged account is a user account with elevated permissions that allows access to critical system resources, applications, and data.

Privileged accounts are typically used by system administrators and other trusted users.

Examples of privileged accounts include root accounts on Unix or Linux and the sa account on SQL Server.

Privileged accounts are used for software installations, updates, system configurations, and managing user accounts.

Privileged accounts are used for managing network resources, security settings, and access controls.

Security considerations for privileged accounts include unauthorized actions and malicious insiders.

Privileged account users are also prone to phishing.

Recommendations for privileged accounts include limiting use to essential tasks and implementing MFA.

Periodic reviews of privileged accounts and their activities should be conducted.

Strong password policies and password management tools should be enforced for privileged accounts.

Discretionary access control allows the owner, creator, or data custodian of an object to control and define access to that object.

All objects have owners, and access control is based on the discretion or decision of the owner.

If a user creates a new spreadsheet file, that user is both the creator of the file and the owner of the file.

The owner can modify the permissions of the file to grant or deny access to other users.

Data owners can delegate day-to-day tasks for handling data to data custodians.

Identity-based access control is a subset of DAC because systems identify users based on their identity.

A DAC model is implemented using access control lists on objects.

Each ACL defines the types of access granted or denied to subjects.

DAC does not offer a centrally controlled management system because owners can alter the ACLs on their objects at will.

Microsoft Windows systems use the DAC model to manage files.

Each file and folder in Windows has an ACL also known as a DACL identifying the permissions granted to any user or group.

Role-based access control defines a subject's ability to access an object based on the subject's job role.

Administrators often implement role-based access control using groups or roles.

RBAC helps enforce the principle of least privilege by preventing privilege creep.

Privilege creep is the tendency for users to accrue privileges over time as their roles and access needs change.

Administrators should revoke user privileges when users change jobs within an organization.

RBAC is useful in dynamic environments with frequent personnel changes.

Users can belong to multiple roles or groups.

Microsoft operating systems implement RBAC with the use of groups.

Some groups such as the local Administrators group are predefined.

Administrators can create additional groups to match the job functions or roles used in an organization.

Rule-based access control uses a set of rules, restrictions, or filters to determine what can and cannot occur on a system.

Rule-based access control includes granting a subject access to an object or granting the subject the ability to perform an action.

Rule-based access control models have global rules that apply to all subjects.

One common example of a rule-based access control model is a firewall.

A firewall examines all the traffic going through it and only allows traffic that meets one of the rules.

Attribute-based access control uses policies that include multiple attributes for rules.

Attributes can be almost any characteristic of users, the network, and devices on the network.

User attributes can include group membership, the department where they work, and devices they use.

The network can be the local internal network, a wireless network, an intranet, or a wide area network.

Devices can include firewalls, proxy servers, web servers, and database servers.

Mandatory access control relies on the use of classification labels which represent a security domain.

A security domain is a collection of subjects and objects that share a common security policy.

A security domain could have the label Secret and the MAC model would protect all objects with the Secret label.

Subjects are only able to access objects with the Secret label when they have a matching Secret label.

Users may have more than one label if they are cleared to access multiple levels of information.

The requirement for subjects to gain the Secret label is the same for all subjects.

Users have labels assigned to them based on their clearance level which is a form of privilege.

Objects have labels which indicate their level of classification or sensitivity.

The U.S. military uses the labels Top Secret, Secret, Confidential, and Unclassified to classify data.

Administrators can grant access to Top Secret data to users with Top Secret clearances.

Administrators cannot grant access to Top Secret data to users with lower-level clearances.

Risk-based access control attempts to evaluate risk by considering the environment, the situation, and security policies.

A security policy in risk-based access control is software code that makes risk-based decisions based on available data.

An organization would modify the choices within the software to support their needs.

Doctors and nurses in an emergency room need access to patient data for any patient who shows up in the ER.

Security policies will likely consider a medical emergency as low risk and grant full access to patient data.

Personnel in the pharmacy department would be granted some access to patient data to identify potential adverse drug interactions.

The risk-based model would prevent the pharmacy from accessing the full medical history of a patient.

A major concern for any IT department is the security and integrity of data or information.

Information can exist in one of two states: at rest or in motion.

Data at rest is data that is in a storage system.

Some devices such as web servers are accessible from outside an organization's network and are open to attack.

Portable devices like smartphones, tablets, flash memory devices, and laptops are prone to physical theft and inadvertent loss.

Data in motion is data that is in transit such as on a network.

Webpage files are considered in motion while they are sent from a web server through the Internet to a web browser.

DIM is vulnerable to improper disclosure and theft.

DIM is vulnerable as it travels over the network whether the network is wired or wireless.

In high-security environments administrators may choose to use a mandatory access control model for data at rest.

Those seeking more flexibility will likely adopt the more common discretionary access control model.

Users of both MAC and DAC models may also choose to implement role-based access control.

Access controls alone are not enough to secure data at rest.

Portable devices can be stolen and access controls can be circumvented.

A malicious user could mount a stolen laptop hard drive on another system on which he or she has administrative rights.

Disk encryption especially whole disk encryption is essential to securing data at rest especially on a portable device.

It is difficult for an unauthorized user to extract data from an encrypted disk.

One way to secure data in motion is to encrypt data which is especially common with email.

The information sender and receiver agree on an encryption technique and then share the appropriate keys.

Anyone who intercepts the data packets in transit is left with meaningless bits of data.

Applying an access control policy to an organization's file system is the first step when securing data at rest.

An access control list handles both rights management of the data and auditing of data access.

File system access controls deal directly with accessing data stored on a system or a group of systems sharing a common file system.

ACLs in a Microsoft Windows environment can be managed using Active Directory.

ACLs in a Linux environment can be managed using built-in ACL capabilities.

A Discretionary Access Control List specifies which users and groups can access a resource and what operations they can perform.

A DACL is an internal list attached to an object such as files, folders, or other resources in systems like Windows.

A System Access Control List is a system-created access control list that handles the information assurance aspect of access controls.

The SACL is a system-generated list based on the auditing rules set by the systems administrators.

Access controls can be applied to applications as well as end users and objects.

You can assign an application its own user ID and directories.

You can grant the application rights only to the files and directories it needs to run and explicitly deny access to the rest of the system.

This gives the application a sandbox to work in.

If the application is compromised through an exploit in its code, the malicious user will only have access to the information that the application had access to.

This type of access control is common on web servers and application servers.

Certain executables have their own internal access controls.

Internal access controls are necessary where the application holds sensitive information in a format that is not accessible to the general file system.

This situation is common in database management systems where data are stored in raw form in tables or in binary large objects.

Information in database management systems must still be secured but is inaccessible to the file system ACLs.

Often this information is secured by implementing an RBAC system built into the application.

Network access control refers to the systems and policies designed to govern access to an organization's network-connected resources.

NAC includes both physical devices like PCs and routers as well as virtualized and cloud-based assets.

NAC solutions focus on enforcing security protocols around who and what can connect to networked systems.

NAC solutions authenticate user identities and ensure devices meet compliance standards before granting admission.

Once admitted users may face additional permissions, restrictions, and monitoring based on their access tier.

There are two primary categories of network access control solutions: pre-admission NAC and post-admission NAC.

Pre-admission NAC solutions focus on evaluating any attempts by a user or device to gain initial access to the network.

Pre-admission NAC tools check the supplied credentials against user directories and access control lists.

Pre-admission NAC tools confirm the requesting endpoint meets security standards through policy checks.

Pre-admission NAC acts as the first line of defense to verify identities and device compliance while blocking unauthorized users.

Multi-factor authentication could be mandated for all external connections within pre-admission NAC policies.

Post-admission NAC refers to controls and monitoring applied to users after they have logged in to the network.

Post-admission NAC is primarily concerned with enforcing on-going restrictions around what authenticated endpoints can access within the network.

Common post-admission NAC capabilities include segmenting the network into zones isolated by internal firewalls.

Post-admission NAC capabilities include continuously tracking user activity and restricting lateral movements between areas.

Post-admission NAC capabilities include automatically disabling suspicious connections.

If malware penetrated a user's device, post-admission NAC would observe the resulting abnormal outbound activity and block it immediately.

NAC is critical for securing bring-your-own-device policies that allow staff to access corporate networks through personal devices.

NAC allows IT staff to onboard external parties quickly through self-service portals with restricted network access.

NAC solutions provide comprehensive device inventories, automate monitoring and management, and enforce consistent policies for IoT networks.

NAC helps secure specialized equipment in industries like energy, manufacturing, and transportation.

NAC improves threat detection while enabling rapid, automated actions like quarantining or disabling impacted systems.

Physical security involves protecting the physical assets and infrastructure that support digital systems.

Without control over the physical environment, no amount of administrative or technical access controls can provide adequate security.

If a malicious person can gain physical access to your facility or equipment, they can do just about anything they want.

A good physical security strategy is designed in layers.

If an intruder bypasses or breaks through one layer, another layer will delay or stop him or her.

Perimeter security is any method that restricts access to a defined area such as a military base, corporate campus, or office building.

Perimeter security is the first layer of defense in a physical security strategy.

A backyard fence is a common example of perimeter security.

Lighting should be designed to illuminate official entry points and those areas that an intruder may use to break in.

Landscape lighting design should avoid creating large areas of shadow that could provide cover for an intruder.

Security guards and closed-captioned video play an important role in perimeter security.

Video cameras placed in such a way that their fields of vision overlap slightly provide the same level of coverage with fewer guards.

Gates of various types allow for monitored, controlled access to a secured facility.

Gates allow for identification and authentication of individuals wishing to gain entry.

Gates restrict the flow of traffic into and out of a facility.

Gates are a weak point in the perimeter so their security should be heightened to compensate.

The mere presence of a security guard at the gate is a deterrent.

A guard can perform identification and authentication tasks by checking ID badges of those wishing to enter.

A guard can strengthen the use of ID cards by physically inspecting the card to ensure that the picture on the ID matches the face of the individual.

Access controls for building security revolve around the concept of the locked door.

A locking mechanism can be as simple as a deadbolt with a key held by the building owner.

A locking mechanism can involve a system that combines ID badges and biometrics.

An intruder often takes the path of least resistance when designing a locking mechanism.

A complex and highly secure lock might not prevent an intruder from entering the building if the intruder can remove the hinges or break a nearby window.

The lock strength must be matched by the integrity of the door and the surrounding area.

Points of entry are weaknesses in any perimeter or building security plan.

Entry points should be well lit and free from heavy landscaping that might provide cover to an intruder.

Security guards posted nearby provide another layer of security at entry and exit points.

Cameras filming entry and exit areas provide a record of who comes and goes.

When designing a physical security system you should be concerned with who enters a facility and also with who leaves.

Potential exit points must be monitored as closely as the official entry points.

Bollards are short vertical posts designed to control traffic and prevent vehicular attacks on a building.

Bollards are designed to allow easy pedestrian access to an area while preventing vehicular access.

Bollards are often installed in areas where terrorist attacks are a concern.

Some bollards are retractable.

Once inside the building, different areas require different security measures.

Generic work areas may not require any additional security systems beyond perimeter and building security.

Sensitive areas such as data centers require additional layers of security.

The primary physical security concern in non-sensitive work areas is suspicious behavior.

Non-sensitive work areas can also be targets for social engineers.

Appropriate lighting, visible security personnel, and surveillance cameras are common ways to secure non-sensitive work areas.

Sensitive work areas are those areas where the general public is not allowed.

A simple badge-based access control system can be used to enter the non-sensitive areas of a facility.

Biometric authentication such as a hand geometry scan can be required before granting access to sensitive areas.

Computer rooms and data centers are specialized forms of sensitive work areas.

Access to computer rooms and data centers should be restricted to a few select individuals.

Because human beings do not ordinarily work in a dark data center, the lights are usually off.

Any data center should have access locks on all server racks and individual systems.

Any data center should have restrictions on electronic devices that can be brought into the data center.

Port locks on servers and racks prevent intruders from downloading data to a portable device by restricting access to USB ports.

Computer rooms and data centers usually have strict policies against bringing in food or drink.

The fewer people who have access to a data center, the more secure it is.

Access control systems such as key card door locks and biometric scanners restrict access to sensitive areas.

Perimeter security includes measures like fences, vehicle barriers, and access control points.

Surveillance systems include CCTV cameras and other surveillance technologies.

Environmental controls include fire suppression systems and HVAC systems.

Physical security personnel include security guards and other personnel who monitor and respond to physical security incidents.

Biometric access controls are those based on physical or behavioral traits.

Biometric systems are essentially sophisticated pattern recognition systems.

Enrollment is the process of taking one or more sample readings from an individual to form an accurate image of that individual's traits.

Verification is the process of taking a reading on an individual and comparing it with previously stored data.

Verification may be an identification process where the individual is checked against a large database of known individuals.

Verification may be an authentication process where the individual makes a claim of identity and the biometric system verifies that claim.

Biometric traits fall into two distinct categories: physical characteristics and behavioral characteristics.

Physiological biometric types are physical attributes that are unique to an individual.

Some traits such as fingerprints are set at birth and do not change over an individual's lifetime.

Others such as facial recognition and hand geometry can change significantly during childhood but are relatively constant during adulthood.

Behavioral biometrics take advantage of many characteristic behaviors that individuals exhibit consistently.

You follow certain rhythms when you type or write your signature.

When you speak you modulate your voice in specific ways that are unique to you.

Behavioral biometrics focus on actions that most people do automatically.

These are subconscious actions that are difficult to change consciously.

Behavioral biometrics can be tricked by someone who takes the time to study a subject's behaviors and learns to imitate them.

Behavioral biometrics are best when used for lower security targets or when used in combination with another biometric or traditional access control method.

Implementing a biometric access control system is more difficult than implementing a traditional access control system.

User acceptance is one common barrier to biometric analysis.

Many users who are unfamiliar with the technology find it intrusive or even frightening to undergo a retinal scan or facial recognition.

Many users have privacy concerns over the idea of their biometric data being stored.

When choosing a biometric access control system weigh the potential for false acceptances and rejections, crossover errors, and failures in the enrollment and capturing processes.

Privilege escalation refers to any situation that gives users more privileges than they should have.

A regular user would have enough privileges to perform their job but no more.

Domain administrators have full rights and permissions within a domain.

Regular users should not have the same privileges as administrators.

Passwords are the weakest form of authentication and there are many types of password attacks.

If an attacker is successful in a password attack, the attacker can access the account and access resources authorized to the account.

If an attacker discovers a root or administrator password, the attacker can access any other account and its resources.

A dictionary attack is an attempt to discover passwords by using every possible password in a predefined database or list of common or expected passwords.

Dictionary attack databases include character combinations widely used as weak passwords but not found in dictionaries.

Passwords such as 123456 and password are found in password-cracking dictionaries.

Dictionary attacks often scan for one-upped-constructed passwords.

A one-upped-constructed password is a previously used password but with one character different.

Password1 is one-upped from password, as are password2, 1password, and passxWord.

A brute-force attack is an attempt to discover passwords for user accounts by systematically attempting all possible combinations of letters, numbers, and symbols.

A hybrid attack attempts a dictionary attack and then performs a type of brute-force attack with one-upped-constructed passwords.

Longer and more complex passwords take more time and are costlier to crack than simple passwords.

The longer the password and the more character types it includes, the more secure it is against brute-force attacks.

A spraying attack is a special type of brute-force attack used in online password attacks.

Spraying attacks attempt to bypass account lockout security controls.

A system will lock out an account if the same user enters the wrong password too many times within a short amount of time.

In a spraying attack, a program uses the same guessed password but loops through a list of different accounts and different systems.

Credential stuffing checks a single username and password on several sites.

Credential stuffing occurs when people use the same credentials on multiple sites.

If people use different passwords on all sites, a credential stuffing attack will fail.

A birthday attack focuses on finding collisions.

The birthday paradox states that if there are 23 people in a room, there is a 50 percent chance that any two of them will have the same birthday.

You can reduce the success of birthday attacks by using hashing algorithms with enough bits and using salts.

MD5 is no longer recommended as a cryptographic hash.

SHA-3 can use as many as 512 bits and is more collision resistant to brute-force attacks than MD5.

A rainbow table reduces the time to find a password by using large databases of precomputed hashes.

Attackers create rainbow tables by guessing a password, hashing the guessed password, and putting both into the rainbow table.

A password cracker can compare every hash in the rainbow table against the hash in a stolen password database file.

When using the rainbow table, the password cracker does not spend any time guessing and calculating hashes.

Many systems commonly salt passwords to reduce the effectiveness of rainbow table attacks.

A salt is a group of random bits added to a password before hashing it.

Argon2, bcrypt, and Password-Based Key Derivation Function 2 are some algorithms used to salt passwords.

A pass-the-hash attack allows an attacker to send a captured hash of a password to an authenticating service.

In a pass-the-hash attack, the attacker does not need to know the actual password.

Pass-the-hash attacks are primarily associated with Windows systems using NT LAN Manager or Kerberos.

The best protection against pass-the-hash attacks is to prevent the infection of the first computer.

Sniffing captures packets sent over a network with the intent of analyzing the packets.

A sniffer is a software application that captures traffic traveling over the network.

A sniffer attack occurs when an attacker uses a sniffer to capture information transmitted over a network.

Attackers can capture and read any data sent over a network in cleartext, including passwords.

Encrypt all sensitive data sent over a network to prevent successful sniffing attacks.

Avoid the use of insecure protocols such as HTTP, FTP, and Telnet.

Use secure protocols such as HTTPS, SFTP, and SSH.

Use one-time passwords when encryption is not possible or feasible.

Protect network devices with physical security to prevent attackers from installing sniffers.

Onion routing and Tor are technologies that aim to provide anonymity and privacy for internet users.

The core principle of Tor known as onion routing was developed in the mid-1990s by United States Naval Research Laboratory employees.

In 2006 Dingledine, Mathewson, and five others founded The Tor Project.

Onion routing is a technique for anonymous communication over a computer network.

Onion routing works by encrypting data multiple times and sending it through a series of network nodes called onion routers.

Each router peels off a layer of encryption revealing the next destination.

Tor is short for The Onion Router and is free and open-source software that implements onion routing.

To begin your data gets broken into codes multiple times and layers in onion routing.

Your encrypted data takes a randomized path through at least three nodes.

Each node can only decrypt the layer they are responsible for to determine the next node.

The exit node decrypts the last layer of encryption and delivers your information to its ultimate destination.

Every node only knows the identities of its immediate neighbors in the chain.

If someone compromises one node they cannot re-identify your traffic or discover where your traffic is going.

Tor is a distributed overlay network designed to anonymize low-latency TCP-based applications.

The network is built of a number of servers called relays.

To connect to the network a client needs to download an up-to-date signed directory of the relays.

Directory documents are generated and signed by a set of semi-trusted directory authority servers.

A channel is an encrypted reliable non-anonymous transport between a client and a relay.

A client chooses a path which is a sequence of relays on the network.

The client uses the channel to build a multi-hop cryptographic structure called a circuit.

A circuit is built over a sequence of relays typically three.

Every relay in the circuit knows its predecessor and successor but no other relays.

Many circuits can be multiplexed over a single channel.

In onion routing data gets wrapped in multiple layers of encryption at the entry node.

Each middle node decrypts one layer of encryption revealing only the destination of the next node.

Only the exit node knows where the message is actually going.

The layers of encryption conceal your IP address from the web pages you visit and your internet service provider.

Each layer of the onion is encrypted with the public key of a specific node.

The onion routing protocol operates on a decentralized network based on thousands of servers called relays.

Mostly individuals who do not require any oversight from an authority run these nodes.

The use of onion routing services such as the Tor network is legal in most countries.

Blockchains are shared, immutable ledgers for recording transactions, tracking assets, and building trust.

A blockchain is a list of records called blocks that are linked cryptographically.

Each block contains a hash of the previous block, the timestamp of the transaction, and the content of the transaction itself.

Because each block depends on the previous one, blocks are logically connected which precludes altering or forging any block.

The hash acts as proof that the transaction took place at a particular time.

The use of a hash function makes it relatively inexpensive to audit and verify transactions.

The transactions of a blockchain are bound together in a structure called a Merkle tree.

In a Merkle tree each pair of transactions at the leaves of the tree is hashed together.

The hashes of these pairs of leaf nodes are hashed then pairs of the pairs and so on until the root contains a hash reflecting all transactions.

Hash values tie all pieces of the blockchain together.

The previous block hash ensures chronological linkage in the blockchain and guarantees chain continuity.

The version component indicates the rule validation protocol.

The difficulty component shows the current mining difficulty value.

The Merkle Root Hash summarizes all transactions in the block.

The timestamp indicates the date and time the block was created.

The Proof of Work or Nonce is the result of the mining process for creating new blocks.

The transactions component is the detailed list of all transactions included in the block.

Blockchain is not just for cryptocurrencies and its basic concept is a chain of transactions agreed by all participants.

The important feature of a blockchain is its transparency where nobody can cheat because everybody computes the hashes.

There is no owner or supreme authority of the chain so it does not depend on any government or superpower.

The purpose of a consensus mechanism is to bring all the nodes in agreement in an environment where the nodes don't trust each other.
