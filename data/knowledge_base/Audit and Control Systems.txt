The two core dimensions of risk analysis are likelihood and impact.

Likelihood or probability is how likely it is that the risk event will occur within a given period.

Impact or consequence or severity is how significant the effect would be if the risk did occur.

Qualitative analysis uses descriptive scales such as Low, Medium, and High based on judgement and experience.

Qualitative analysis is faster and more common for initial screening but more subjective.

Quantitative analysis uses numerical or statistical data to calculate risk more precisely.

Quantitative analysis is more objective but requires reliable data, which is not always available.

Many organisations use a semi-quantitative approach with numerical scoring scales applied to qualitative judgements.

The semi-quantitative approach typically feeds a risk matrix.

Inherent risk is the level of risk that exists before considering any controls in place.

Residual risk is the level of risk that remains after controls are applied.

Control risk is the risk that a control fails to prevent or detect a material issue.

Auditors are especially interested in residual risk because it represents the actual exposure the organisation is carrying today.

Understanding inherent risk tells the auditor how much the controls actually matter.

A risk matrix or risk heat map is a visual tool that plots likelihood against impact.

A risk matrix gives each identified risk an overall risk rating.

A risk matrix makes it easy to compare and prioritise risks at a glance.

Each identified risk is scored for likelihood and impact and plotted onto the matrix.

The resulting rating such as Low, Medium, High, or Critical drives prioritisation of audit effort.

Critical and High risk areas receive more frequent and detailed audit attention.

The matrix supports communication with non-technical stakeholders such as the board and audit committee.

A visual heat map is often far more effective than a long narrative list of risks.

Materiality is the concept of significance.

Materiality is the threshold at which an error, weakness, or omission becomes important enough to influence the decisions of someone relying on the audit's conclusions.

Materiality helps auditors decide what genuinely matters enough to focus on versus what can reasonably be left out of scope.

In financial audit, materiality is often expressed quantitatively as a percentage of revenue or assets.

In IT and IS audit, materiality is frequently more qualitative.

The impact of a weak IT control is not always a direct dollar figure.

Materiality factors include sensitivity and criticality of the data or system involved.

Materiality factors include potential impact on confidentiality, integrity, or availability.

Materiality factors include regulatory or contractual significance.

A GDPR-relevant system carries inherent materiality regardless of size.

Materiality factors include reputational exposure.

Materiality factors include whether the issue could mask or enable a more significant financial or operational failure.

Weak access controls over the general ledger system are material even though the control itself is not a financial figure.

Audits have limited time and resources so materiality helps the auditor decide where to focus fieldwork.

Materiality shapes reporting by determining what belongs in headline findings versus a lower-level management letter.

Not every minor observation needs to be escalated to the board.

Materiality connects directly to risk analysis.

A highly material area combined with high likelihood of failure should receive the greatest audit attention.

Strategic or annual audit planning develops the overall audit plan or schedule for the year or multi-year cycle.

Strategic audit planning is based on the risk-ranked audit universe.

Strategic audit planning determines which areas get audited and how often.

Engagement-level planning is planning for a single specific audit once it has been selected from the annual plan.

Engagement-level planning determines the objectives, scope, and approach for that particular audit.

The audit planning process begins with understanding the business or area to be audited.

The audit planning process includes identifying and assessing risk.

The audit planning process includes determining materiality to guide scope and testing depth.

The audit planning process includes defining audit objectives and scope.

Defining scope includes stating what is explicitly out of scope.

The audit planning process includes identifying criteria such as ISO/IEC 27001, COBIT, or internal policy.

The audit planning process includes determining resources and timeline including staffing, skills, budget, and scheduling.

The audit planning process includes developing the audit programme of detailed procedures and tests.

The audit planning process includes communicating with the auditee and holding an opening or entry meeting.

Poor planning is one of the most common root causes of ineffective audits.

Poor planning can lead to testing the wrong things, missing material risks, or running out of time and resources mid-engagement.

Good planning grounded in solid risk assessment allows an audit to deliver real assurance rather than a superficial checklist exercise.

At the strategic or annual level, an audit plan includes a list of auditable areas from the audit universe with assigned risk ratings.

At the strategic or annual level, an audit plan includes a prioritised schedule of planned audits with proposed timing and frequency.

At the strategic or annual level, an audit plan includes resource allocation across the plan including staffing, budget, and specialist needs.

At the strategic or annual level, an audit plan requires approval by the audit committee or board.

At the individual engagement level, an audit plan includes audit objectives.

At the individual engagement level, an audit plan includes scope, which is the boundaries of the audit covering systems, processes, locations, and time period.

At the individual engagement level, an audit plan includes criteria, which are the standards, frameworks, or policies being tested against.

At the individual engagement level, an audit plan includes a risk assessment summary of key risks and their ratings.

At the individual engagement level, an audit plan includes materiality thresholds for what will be considered significant enough to report.

At the individual engagement level, an audit plan includes the audit approach or methodology such as interviews, control testing, data analytics, and sampling.

At the individual engagement level, an audit plan includes timeline and milestones for planning, fieldwork, reporting, and follow-up.

At the individual engagement level, an audit plan includes roles and responsibilities for who performs and who reviews the audit.

At the individual engagement level, an audit plan includes reporting requirements specifying to whom, in what format, and by when.

An audit plan is not fixed in stone.

If fieldwork reveals new risks or information not known at the planning stage, scope may need to be revisited.

This reflects the risk-based, adaptive nature of modern auditing.

The overall flow is to identify risks, analyse and rate them, visualise and prioritise via a risk matrix, apply materiality, plan the audit, and document it in a formal audit plan.

An audit engagement is a single, defined piece of audit work with its own objectives, scope, timeline, and team.

An audit engagement is carried out within the broader annual audit plan.

An engagement letter or terms of reference is a formal document confirming objectives, scope, responsibilities, timeline, and reporting lines before work begins.

Engagement letters are particularly common for external or outsourced audits.

Notification to the auditee informs the area or department being audited about logistics and expected involvement.

An opening or entry meeting is a kickoff meeting with the auditee.

The opening meeting confirms scope, introduces the audit team, agrees access and logistics, and sets expectations for communication.

The engagement lead or audit manager has overall responsibility for the engagement, quality, and client relationships.

Audit team members perform fieldwork, testing, and evidence gathering.

The reviewer or quality assurance role reviews work for quality and adherence to standards before conclusions are finalised.

Auditee or process owners provide access, information, and responses to findings.

An engagement formally closes once the report is issued, management responses are obtained, and follow-up on remediation is scheduled.

The engagement itself ends at reporting but accountability for the risk continues until issues are resolved.

An audit programme is also called an audit work programme.

An audit programme is the detailed set of procedures and tests to be performed during an engagement to achieve its objectives.

The audit plan says what will be audited and why, while the audit programme says exactly how the auditor will test it step by step.

An audit programme translates high-level audit objectives into specific, executable test procedures.

An audit programme ensures consistency so different team members performing similar tests do so the same way.

An audit programme provides a basis for supervision and review.

An audit programme creates a record of what was actually tested, supporting audit documentation.

For each control or risk area being tested, the programme includes the objective of the specific test.

For each control or risk area, the programme includes the precise procedure steps the auditor will follow.

For each control or risk area, the programme includes sample size and selection method where applicable.

For each control or risk area, the programme includes the evidence to be obtained such as screenshots, system logs, signed approvals, and configuration exports.

For each control or risk area, the programme includes the criteria or standard the result will be judged against.

For each control or risk area, the programme includes space for results, conclusion, and sign-off by the preparer and reviewer.

Many audit functions maintain a library of standard or template programmes for common areas such as access management, change management, and backup and recovery.

Standard programmes improve efficiency and consistency.

Programmes should always be tailored to the specific engagement's risk assessment.

A template is a starting point, not a substitute for professional judgement about what actually matters in a particular audit.

Audit functions typically operate with limited staff, time, and budget relative to the size of the audit universe.

How resources are allocated directly determines audit quality and coverage.

Resource allocation is a core audit management responsibility distinct from risk prioritisation.

Resource considerations include people, meaning the number of auditors and the right mix of skills.

Resource considerations include time, meaning hours or days budgeted for each phase and engagement.

Resource considerations include budget, meaning costs such as travel, specialist external resources, and tools.

Resource considerations include tools and technology such as audit management software, data analytics platforms, and security testing tools.

Factors affecting resource allocation include the risk rating of the area, where higher risk generally warrants more resources and more experienced staff.

Factors affecting resource allocation include complexity of the systems or processes involved.

Factors affecting resource allocation include required expertise, such as a cloud security audit needing a platform specialist.

Factors affecting resource allocation include independence requirements to ensure staff assigned have no conflicts of interest.

Factors affecting resource allocation include competing priorities across the annual audit plan.

Where internal skills are insufficient, organisations may co-source by bringing in external specialists to work alongside internal staff.

Where internal skills are insufficient, organisations may outsource by fully delegating an audit engagement to an external firm.

Both co-sourcing and outsourcing still require internal oversight to maintain accountability and quality.

Scheduling is the process of sequencing and timing audit engagements across the audit period.

Scheduling converts the prioritised audit plan into an actual, realistic calendar of work.

Scheduling factors include risk ranking, where higher-risk areas are typically scheduled earlier or more frequently.

Scheduling factors include resource availability such as staff capacity and seasonal workload peaks.

Scheduling factors include business cycles, such as auditing a system shortly after a major upgrade or migration.

Scheduling factors include regulatory deadlines such as ISO/IEC 27001 surveillance audits.

Scheduling factors include dependencies between engagements, such as an IT general controls audit needing completion before certain application-level audits.

Practical scheduling tools include Gantt charts and audit calendars showing planned engagements across the year.

Practical scheduling tools include audit management software tracking engagement status, staff allocation, and deadlines in real time.

Schedules must remain flexible, consistent with the risk-based and adaptive approach.

An emerging risk such as a new vulnerability disclosure, regulatory change, or incident may require re-prioritising the schedule mid-cycle.

Re-prioritising may delay lower-risk planned work.

An audit checklist is a structured list of items, questions, or controls to be verified during an audit.

An audit checklist supports consistent and complete testing.

Checklists help ensure completeness so nothing important is accidentally overlooked.

Checklists are especially useful for less experienced auditors or high-volume routine checks.

Checklists support consistency across auditors and engagements.

Checklists provide a quick, structured record of what was checked and the outcome.

Checklists are useful as a training tool for new audit staff.

An audit programme is broader than a checklist and defines objectives, procedures, sample sizes, and evidence requirements.

An audit programme requires professional judgement to execute.

A checklist is typically a simpler, binary or verification-style tool using yes or no and present or absent.

Checklists are often used within the execution of a programme or for straightforward compliance-style checks.

Over-reliance on checklists can encourage a tick-box mentality.

A tick-box mentality means auditors confirm an item exists without evaluating whether it is genuinely effective.

Checklists may not adapt well to unusual, complex, or evolving risks, particularly in fast-changing IT and cyber environments.

Checklists should be seen as a support tool, not a replacement for professional scepticism and judgement.

Even a well-planned, well-resourced engagement can fail without effective day-to-day team management.

Key responsibilities of an audit manager include assigning work by matching tasks to team members' skills and experience.

Key responsibilities of an audit manager include supervision by reviewing work in progress, not just at the end.

Key responsibilities of an audit manager include communication by keeping the team, auditee, and stakeholders informed of progress, issues, and scope changes.

Key responsibilities of an audit manager include quality control to ensure documentation, evidence, and conclusions meet required standards.

Key responsibilities of an audit manager include managing conflict and objections when the auditee disagrees with findings.

Managing conflict requires firm but professional negotiation grounded in evidence.

Key responsibilities of an audit manager include developing staff by coaching junior auditors and supporting professional development toward certifications such as CISA.

The audit manager must ensure no team member has a conflict of interest with the area being audited.

The audit manager must protect the team from undue pressure or influence from the auditee, including pressure directed at junior staff.

Team composition considerations include a balance of experience levels with senior oversight and junior staff performing detailed testing under supervision.

Team composition considerations include the right mix of technical and specialist skills for the specific engagement.

Team composition considerations include reasonable workload distribution to avoid burnout and maintain quality.

Rushed audits are more likely to miss material issues.

Communication throughout the engagement includes regular status updates to the auditee and audit committee or sponsor, especially for longer engagements.

Early escalation of significant findings is good practice rather than saving up major issues for the final report.

Serious risks should be flagged as soon as they are confirmed.

Communication includes managing the exit or closing meeting professionally.

The exit meeting should ensure findings are clearly understood and management responses are captured accurately.

Audit evidence is the information gathered by an auditor to support the conclusions and opinions reached during an audit.

Every finding, conclusion, and line in an audit report must be traceable back to evidence.

Physical evidence is obtained through direct observation of assets, processes, or people.

An example of physical evidence is observing a data centre's physical access controls in operation.

Documentary evidence includes records, policies, logs, contracts, system-generated reports, screenshots, and configuration exports.

Testimonial evidence is information obtained through interviews or written representations from staff and management.

Analytical evidence is derived from analysing and comparing data.

Examples of analytical evidence include trend analysis, ratio analysis, and exception reporting.

Analytical evidence is often produced using CAATs.

Internal evidence is generated within the organisation being audited.

Examples of internal evidence include internal logs, policies, and meeting minutes.

External evidence is obtained from outside the organisation.

Examples of external evidence include confirmation letters from third parties, vendor certifications, and regulatory correspondence.

External evidence is generally considered more reliable since it is less subject to the auditee's influence.

Inspection is the examination of records, documents, or physical assets to gather evidence.

Inspection is one of the most commonly used audit techniques because it produces tangible, verifiable evidence.

Inspection examples include reviewing system configuration settings against a security baseline.

Inspection examples include examining access control lists, user account listings, or firewall rule sets.

Inspection examples include reviewing signed change-approval forms or ticketing system records.

Inspection examples include inspecting physical server room access logs or badge systems.

Inspection examples include reviewing policy documents to confirm they exist, are current, and are approved.

A strength of inspection is that it produces objective, often reproducible evidence.

A strength of inspection is that documents can usually be re-examined by a reviewer.

A limitation of inspection is that it confirms a document or record exists but does not confirm the underlying control is operating effectively.

A signed policy does not prove staff actually follow it.

Inspection is often combined with other techniques.

Observation involves the auditor watching a process, procedure, or control being performed in real time by the people who normally perform it.

An observation example is watching an IT administrator perform user account provisioning to confirm it follows the documented procedure.

An observation example is observing physical entry controls at a data centre to see whether tailgating is prevented.

An observation example is observing a backup or incident response drill or simulation.

An observation example is watching how a helpdesk handles a password reset request to check identity verification steps are followed.

A strength of observation is that it provides direct, real-time evidence of how a process actually works.

A limitation of observation is the observer effect, sometimes called the Hawthorne effect.

People may behave more carefully than usual because they know they are being watched by an auditor.

Observation only captures a single point in time, not the entire period under review.

Interviews are also referred to as inquiry.

Interviews involve asking questions of management, staff, or other relevant parties to obtain information, explanations, or representations.

Interviews are used to understand how a process or control is supposed to work.

Interviews are used to clarify findings or unusual results identified through other techniques.

Interviews are used to gather context that documents alone cannot provide.

Context from interviews may include informal workarounds, known pain points, and historical incidents not formally logged.

Open-ended questions encourage detailed explanation.

Closed or specific questions are used to confirm precise facts.

Follow-up or probing questions are used to test consistency and dig deeper into vague or evasive answers.

A strength of interviews is that they are flexible and can quickly surface issues that would not appear in documentation.

Interviews are useful for understanding intent and context.

A limitation of interviews is that testimonial evidence is generally considered the weakest and least reliable form of evidence on its own.

Testimonial evidence relies on the interviewee's memory, honesty, and perspective.

Staff being audited may have an incentive to present things favourably.

Interview evidence should typically be corroborated with inspection, observation, or system-derived evidence.

Auditors rarely have the time or resources to test one hundred percent of a population.

Sampling allows auditors to test a representative subset and draw reasonable conclusions about the whole population.

Statistical sampling uses mathematical or probability-based methods to select a sample.

Statistical sampling can support a quantifiable conclusion about the error rate in the full population.

Random sampling means every item in the population has an equal chance of selection.

Systematic sampling means selecting every nth item from the population.

Non-statistical or judgmental sampling uses professional judgement to select items, often based on risk.

Judgmental or risk-based sampling deliberately targets higher-risk items.

Examples of higher-risk items include unusually large transactions, privileged user accounts, and changes made outside normal business hours.

Haphazard sampling selects items without any structured technique, attempting to avoid bias.

Haphazard sampling lacks the statistical rigor of random sampling.

Population is the complete set of items being tested.

Sample size is how many items are selected.

Sample size is influenced by materiality, the risk level of the area, and the desired confidence level.

Sampling risk is the risk that the auditor's conclusion based on the sample differs from the conclusion from testing the entire population.

CAATs are software tools and techniques used by auditors to analyse data, test controls, and gather evidence electronically.

CAATs reduce reliance solely on manual inspection or small samples.

CAATs represent where technical skill and audit practice directly intersect.

Data extraction and analysis tools are used to extract and analyse full data populations and identify anomalies, duplicates, or exceptions.

Examples of data extraction and analysis tools include ACL, IDEA, SQL queries, Excel or Power Query, and Python scripts.

Generalised Audit Software is purpose-built audit analytics platforms enabling recalculation, stratification, and trend analysis across entire datasets.

Test data techniques involve auditors submitting specially designed test transactions into a system to confirm it processes them correctly.

An example of test data is testing whether a system correctly rejects an invalid transaction.

Continuous auditing or monitoring tools provide automated, ongoing testing of controls and transactions in near real-time.

Vulnerability scanning and configuration assessment tools are technical tools used in IT and security audits to assess system configurations against security baselines.

Scripting involves auditors writing their own scripts such as Python or PowerShell to automate repetitive data-pulling or analysis tasks.

A benefit of CAATs is enabling testing of entire populations rather than small samples, removing sampling risk.

A benefit of CAATs is increased efficiency and consistency, especially for large datasets.

A benefit of CAATs is identifying patterns, anomalies, and exceptions that manual review would likely miss.

A benefit of CAATs is supporting continuous or ongoing auditing rather than only periodic point-in-time reviews.

A limitation of CAATs is that they require technical skill to use correctly.

A badly written query or script can produce misleading results with false confidence.

Data extraction itself must be controlled and verified.

The auditor must confirm the data extracted is complete and unaltered.

Tools support evidence gathering but do not replace professional judgement in interpreting results.

CAATs may require cooperation and access from IT teams to extract data safely without disrupting live systems.

Not all evidence is equally trustworthy.

Audit conclusions are only as strong as the evidence behind them.

Auditors must critically evaluate how much weight to place on each piece of evidence gathered.

External evidence is generally more reliable than internal evidence.

Evidence from an independent third party is less subject to manipulation by the auditee.

Evidence obtained directly by the auditor is more reliable than evidence obtained indirectly or by inference.

An example is that the auditor personally extracting system logs is more reliable than being told what the logs show.

Documentary evidence is generally more reliable than oral evidence.

Written or system-recorded evidence typically leaves a verifiable trail, while interviews alone do not.

Evidence from a well-controlled environment is more reliable than evidence from a poorly controlled one.

Data extracted from a system with strong access and change controls is more trustworthy than data from a system with weak integrity controls.

Original documents are more reliable than copies because copies can be altered without easy detection.

The rough hierarchy from most to least reliable begins with the auditor's own direct observation or testing of a well-controlled system.

An example of the most reliable evidence is CAAT-based analysis of a full data population.

The second most reliable is external, independently sourced documentary evidence.

Examples of external documentary evidence include third-party certification and bank confirmation.

The third most reliable is internal documentary evidence from a well-controlled source system.

An example is system-generated logs from a system with strong integrity controls.

The fourth most reliable is internal documentary evidence from a weakly controlled source.

The least reliable is oral or testimonial evidence from interviews, especially if uncorroborated.

Auditors assess evidence against two combined criteria echoing ISACA standards.

Sufficiency asks whether there is enough evidence to support the conclusion.

Appropriateness asks whether the evidence is relevant and reliable enough for the specific objective being tested.

Weaker evidence such as a single interview can still be used but typically needs corroboration by additional, more reliable evidence before a significant conclusion is drawn from it.
