The Mauritian legal system is a unique hybrid blending elements of French civil law and British common law.

The dual heritage stems from the island's colonial past under French rule from 1715 to 1810 and British rule from 1810 to 1968.

French influence is primarily seen in the substantive law, particularly the Code Napoléon.

British influence is evident in procedural aspects including court structure, evidence, and language.

The Code Napoléon forms the basis of much of Mauritius's civil law including contract law, property law, and civil procedure.

The Code de Commerce governs commercial activities.

The Code Pénal defines criminal offenses and punishments.

British common law principles were adopted particularly in procedure, evidence, and court organization.

The British introduced English as the language of the courts and legal documents.

The British established a court hierarchy with the Supreme Court of Mauritius as the highest court of the island.

The British introduced legislative processes and parliamentary systems.

French civil law principles govern the substance of the law and British common law influences procedural aspects.

Mauritius has developed its own jurisprudence adapting and interpreting laws from both traditions.

The legal system continues to evolve with new legislation and judicial precedents.

The Constitution is also known as supreme law.

Any law inconsistent with the Constitution shall to the extent of the inconsistency be void.

Any person aggrieved that a provision of the Constitution has been infringed can apply to the Supreme Court to obtain redress.

Section 45(1) of the Constitution vests law-making power with Parliament.

Parliament may pass laws for the peace, order and good government of Mauritius.

Acts passed by Parliament are referred to as Primary Legislation.

The process begins with an idea often from a government minister which is developed into a draft bill by the responsible ministry.

The drafted bill is referred to the State Law Office for legal scrutiny and to ensure it conforms with the Constitution.

The bill must be approved by the Cabinet before it can be introduced to Parliament.

The Minister responsible moves that the Bill be read for the first time.

The Clerk of the National Assembly reads the short title of the Bill.

No debate takes place at the first reading stage.

At the second reading a motion is made for the Bill to be read for a second time.

Members of Parliament hold a general debate on the rationale and general philosophies of the Bill.

Debates at second reading should focus on broad principles without going into details and technicalities.

No amendments take place at second reading but notice for proposed amendment may be given.

At the Committee Stage the Bill is read thoroughly clause by clause.

A detailed examination of the Bill is carried out by a Committee of the whole House or a Select Committee.

Amendments to the Bill can take place at the Committee Stage.

At the Report stage the bill whether amended or not is finally reported to the National Assembly.

At the Third Reading the Bill is reviewed in its final form after amendments if any.

Exceptionally with the leave of the Speaker a debate can take place on restricted issues at Third Reading.

Debates at the Third Reading stage are extremely rare in Mauritius.

By virtue of section 53 of the Constitution the bill should be voted by a majority of members of parliament present.

For a Bill to become an Act of Parliament it must obtain the Presidential Assent.

The President can withhold his assent and send the Bill back to the Assembly within 21 days with proposed amendments.

Even after obtaining President's assent an Act does not become enforceable until it is published in the Government Gazette.

It is the President who shall cause the Act to be published.

Blockchain is a decentralized, immutable digital ledger that records transactions across a network of computers.

Cryptocurrency is a digital asset that uses blockchain technology for secure, intermediary-free transactions.

Blockchain is the secure infrastructure or ledger while cryptocurrency is a key application acting as a digital medium of exchange.

Cryptocurrency is the most visible use of blockchain technology.

Blockchain is used in smart contracts, supply chain tracking, digital IDs, and Decentralized Finance (DeFi).

Blockchain enables faster and cheaper cross-border transactions compared to traditional banking.

A blockchain is a shared, digital record book that stores information across a network of computers rather than in one central location.

Data is stored in blocks which are linked together in a chronological chain.

This makes it nearly impossible to change or cheat the system.

It provides transparency and trust without needing a middleman.

A digital ledger can be imagined as a Google Sheet that everyone in a network can view and add to but no one can delete or edit past entries.

Transactions are grouped into blocks.

Once a block is full it is cryptographically linked to the previous block creating a secure, unchangeable chain.

Instead of one bank holding a database thousands of computers (nodes) keep a copy ensuring if one fails the data remains safe.

Once data is recorded it is permanent.

Tampering with one block would require changing all subsequent blocks which is nearly impossible.

Before a new block is added the network must agree that the transactions are valid creating trust.

Blockchain uses hashes to link blocks together.

Common uses of blockchain include cryptocurrencies enabling peer-to-peer transfers such as Bitcoin.

Common uses include supply chain tracking goods from source to store.

Common uses include smart contracts which are self-executing contracts automatically triggered without an intermediary.

There are four main types of blockchain networks categorized by access control and governance.

A public blockchain is a completely decentralized, permissionless, and open network where anyone can join, validate transactions, and view the ledger.

Bitcoin is an example of a public blockchain.

A private blockchain is a restrictive, invitation-only network managed by a single organization behind a firewall.

A private blockchain is restricted and centralized.

A consortium blockchain is a semi-decentralized network governed by a group of organizations rather than one entity.

A consortium blockchain is semi-decentralized and multi-organization.

A hybrid blockchain is a combination of private and public blockchain.

A hybrid blockchain features a private, permissioned system alongside a public, permissionless one to manage which data is public and which is confidential.

These types vary in speed, security, and privacy suited for use cases from cryptocurrencies to corporate supply chains.
