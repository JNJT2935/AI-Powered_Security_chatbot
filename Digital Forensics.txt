Digital forensics is the systematic application of scientific and investigative methods to identify, preserve, examine, analyse and communicate information of potential evidential value from digital sources.

This definition reflects NIST's emphasis on proper authority, chain of custody, validation, repeatability, reporting, and expert testimony where relevant.

Digital forensics is not limited to criminal prosecution.

Cybersecurity monitoring detects abnormal or harmful activity and may create alerts and logs that become evidence sources.

Incident response helps contain, eradicate and recover from an incident while forensic methods help preserve facts.

e-Discovery identifies and produces information relevant to legal proceedings and may use collection and processing methods.

Threat intelligence helps understand adversaries, capabilities and patterns but is not automatically proof of a specific event.

Digital forensics is not unrestricted searching because the investigation must remain within authorised scope.

Digital forensics is not simply running a forensic tool because trained investigators evaluate their meaning and limitations.

Digital forensics is not confirmation of an initial accusation because investigators should test alternative explanations.

Digital forensics is not perfect reconstruction because evidence may be incomplete, overwritten, encrypted, altered or distributed.

Digital forensics is not legal advice because investigators must work with appropriate legal, compliance and management authorities.

A cyberattack is a deliberate attempt to compromise the confidentiality, integrity or availability of systems, services or data.

A cybersecurity event is an observable occurrence in a system or network that may be benign, suspicious or harmful.

A cybersecurity incident is an event or series of events that actually or potentially harms operations, assets or security objectives.

Cybercrime is conduct involving digital systems that constitutes an offence under applicable law.

Digital evidence is digital information of potential probative value that may support or challenge a proposition under investigation.

An artefact is a data item or pattern created by user, application, operating-system or network activity.

An unusual event is not automatically an attack and an attack is not automatically a proven crime.

A cyberattack affects people, devices, accounts, applications and networks and may alter system state or generate records.

Traces become potential evidence only when they are relevant to an investigative question and handled under appropriate authority.

Investigators define propositions or questions and then seek information that can support, weaken or refine them.

Phishing and social engineering can be evidenced by email headers, mail logs, URLs, browser history, identity logs and user reports.

Credential compromise can be evidenced by authentication logs, session records, mailbox or cloud audit logs and endpoint artefacts.

Malware and ransomware can be evidenced by endpoint alerts, files, process records, memory, persistence artefacts and network telemetry.

Web-application attacks can be evidenced by web, application, database, identity and network logs and configuration records.

Insider misuse can be evidenced by access logs, file activity, email, removable-media records, approvals and business context.

Data exfiltration can be evidenced by endpoint, proxy, firewall, DNS, cloud-sharing, email, removable-media and data-loss-prevention records.

Denial of service can be evidenced by network telemetry, firewall and load-balancer logs, system metrics and service monitoring.

Digital evidence is fragile and changeable because normal system use can alter timestamps, logs, memory and temporary data.

Digital evidence is exactly reproducible in some contexts because a verified forensic image can permit examination of a duplicate rather than the source.

Digital evidence is partly volatile because some information exists only while a system is running or for a short retention period.

Digital evidence is distributed because relevant records may exist across endpoints, networks, identity systems and cloud services.

Digital evidence is high-volume because a single device or service may hold millions of records.

Digital evidence is context-dependent because a record may be ambiguous without system configuration, time zone and surrounding events.

Digital evidence is duplicate but not automatically authentic because copying bits does not by itself prove source, meaning or completeness.

Digital evidence is human- and machine-generated because artefacts may arise from users, automated tasks, applications or attackers.

Data at rest is stored information such as files on a drive, database records or cloud objects.

Data in use is information being processed including active applications, memory contents and open sessions.

Data in transit is information moving across a network or between services.

Residual or deleted data is information no longer visible through normal interfaces but potentially recoverable.

Digital information is valuable when it helps answer a relevant question and its origin, integrity and meaning can be explained.

A timestamp may appear precise but still require knowledge of time zones, clock drift, application behaviour and whether the field represents creation, modification, access or synchronisation.

Tool output must be interpreted, not merely reproduced.

Computer and endpoint sources include storage media, operating-system artefacts, applications, memory and security telemetry.

Network sources include packet captures, flow records, DNS, proxy, firewall and VPN logs.

Mobile sources include device storage, applications, communications, media, sensors and synchronised services.

Cloud and identity sources include audit records, object histories, authentication, permissions and administrative events.

Email and collaboration sources include message content and metadata, headers, mailbox audit, sharing and meeting records.

The file system records the structures that make files appear simple to users.

Storage layers include physical or logical sector, partition or volume, cluster, file record and directory path.

A sector is a small addressable storage unit used to read exact locations and image media.

A partition or volume is a defined storage region used to identify boundaries and file-system type.

A cluster is a file-system allocation unit used to trace allocated, slack and unallocated space.

A file record is metadata that describes a file and can recover names, times, size and storage references.

A directory path provides human-readable organisation and connects a record to user activity and context.

A forensic image preserves addressable data but the examiner still needs file-system rules to interpret it.

FAT and exFAT organise removable media efficiently.

A FAT directory entry stores a file name, attributes, timestamps, size and first cluster reference.

Allocation information tracks whether clusters are free, allocated or linked as part of a file.

exFAT also uses an allocation bitmap.

The data area contains file content in clusters and old content may remain after deletion until reuse.

Before deletion a directory entry points to the first cluster and allocation information links the cluster chain.

After deletion the directory entry changes and clusters become available for reuse while content may remain until overwritten.

Recovery confidence falls when a file was fragmented, clusters were reused or flash storage behaviour removed old content.

NTFS keeps several views of activity including $MFT, $Bitmap, $LogFile and $UsnJrnl.

$MFT contains file and directory records.

$Bitmap tracks allocated and free clusters.

$LogFile provides file-system transaction support.

$UsnJrnl contains change journal entries when enabled.

Directory indexes provide directory membership and lookup.

One event can appear in several NTFS structures each with a different purpose and retention pattern.

An MFT record may reveal identity through record number, sequence information and file names.

An MFT record may reveal time through metadata timestamps from more than one attribute set.

An MFT record may reveal storage through resident content or references to non-resident data runs.

An MFT record may reveal state through allocated or deleted status, attributes and flags.

An MFT record may show that a file existed in a synchronised folder but cannot alone identify who placed it there.

NTFS timestamps include Created, Modified, MFT changed and Accessed.

Created records when this instance of the file was created in its current context.

Modified records when file content last changed according to recorded metadata.

MFT changed records when file-system metadata for the record changed.

Access recording depends on system behaviour and configuration.

Copying, moving, extraction, synchronisation and applications can update timestamps differently.

Timestamps should be treated as recorded events that require corroboration not as direct proof of a user's action.

FAT family is typically used for older and small removable media.

exFAT is typically used for modern removable media.

NTFS is typically used for Windows system and data volumes.

FAT uses cluster chains while exFAT uses a bitmap plus allocation structures and NTFS uses $Bitmap and attribute data runs.

Metadata depth is relatively limited in FAT, more modern in exFAT and rich in NTFS with attributes, permissions and journals.

Deleted-file analysis uses directory and cluster state in FAT, entry sets and bitmap in exFAT and MFT records in NTFS.

The file-system type changes the examination plan and the meaning of recovered artefacts.

Deleted file record, unallocated space and file slack are not the same.

A deleted file record means the file system marks the file as deleted or releases its allocation and metadata may remain.

Unallocated space consists of clusters currently available for reuse that may contain remnants from earlier files.

File slack is the unused bytes between a file's logical end and the end of its final allocated cluster.

Recovered content needs validation and location alone does not prove ownership, knowledge, intent or the time of deletion.

Autopsy concepts appear in Data Sources, File Views, Results and Timeline.

Data Sources confirm the image and volume structure before interpreting files.

File Views browse file-system paths, deleted files, file types and user-defined views.

Results review artefacts produced by enabled ingest modules and note module limitations.

Timeline explores recorded events while checking time zone, source and artefact type.

Autopsy displays interpretations and the examiner must still verify the source, understand the artefact and record limitations.

First response, file system analysis and reporting connect through control of access, interpretation of metadata and separation of observation from inference.

The integrity of the conclusion begins with the first action at the scene.

The purpose of forensic acquisition is to preserve addressable data, protect the original and make the process reviewable.

A sound acquisition preserves data and the explanation of how the examiner obtained it.

Planning considers authority, access, capacity, volatility and service impact before imaging begins.

Acquisition scope and source identification requires recording device type, make, model, serial number, interface, capacity, condition and encryption state.

The authorised content states whether the acquisition covers an entire physical device, one volume, selected logical files or volatile data.

Dependencies may include credentials, recovery keys, cloud synchronisation, external storage and software needed to interpret the source.

Personal folders remain outside scope unless later evidence and additional authority justify examination.

Pre-acquisition checks confirm written authority, evidence identifiers and the questions the acquisition must support.

Pre-acquisition checks verify the write blocker, cables, power, clean destination media and available storage capacity.

Pre-acquisition checks record tool version, acquisition options, system clock, time zone and expected image format.

Pre-acquisition checks plan for read errors, failing media, encryption, interruption and secure storage after acquisition.

A checklist supports consistency but the examiner still records case-specific decisions.

Live or staged acquisition is used when shutdown may remove encryption access, active sessions or volatile information.

Every live action changes the system and must be justified.

Static acquisition is used when the device can be powered down safely and storage can be accessed through controlled forensic hardware or software.

Documentation must record who authorised the method, the risks considered, the expected evidence gain and the changes introduced.

A physical image captures the whole addressable device but may require more time and storage.

A logical acquisition captures selected files or accessible data but may omit deleted or inaccessible content.

A live acquisition is used for encrypted or active systems but changes the running system.

A remote acquisition is authorised network collection but depends on connectivity and endpoint control.

Method choice must reflect the investigative question, source condition and known technical limitations.

Write protection options include hardware write blockers, software write protection, direct live access and post-acquisition control.

Write protection reduces avoidable change but does not replace documentation or verification.

E01 or expert-witness format supports embedded metadata, segmentation, compression and integrity checks subject to tool implementation.

A logical evidence container stores selected files and related metadata when full physical acquisition is unavailable or unnecessary.

The chosen format should preserve required data, support validation and remain readable by examination tools.

Destination media considerations include capacity, condition, security and operational resilience.

Capacity should allow space for the image, verification data, working copies, exports and temporary processing files.

Destination media should be approved, prepared, labelled and checked according to procedure.

Access should be restricted, suitable encryption used where authorised and custody records maintained.

Stable power, reliable interfaces and monitored storage reduce interruption and image corruption.

Insufficient capacity and unstable storage can invalidate an otherwise correct acquisition plan.

The forensic imaging workflow stages include source, acquisition, verification and recording.

Each imaging stage produces records that another examiner should be able to review.

Integrity verification with cryptographic hashes occurs at acquisition, after acquisition and after transfer.

A matching hash supports data consistency between measurements but does not prove lawful collection, authorship or intent.

When an encrypted laptop remains logged in and the recovery key is unavailable a safe next action must be authorised and documented.

Windows records activity across the file system, Registry, event logs and application artefacts.

Each Windows source has a purpose and a limitation.

Windows context includes system, user, event and file context.

System context includes Windows version, computer name, time settings, installed services and configured accounts.

User context includes profiles, recent items, application activity and settings associated with an account.

Event context includes logon, process, service, device and audit records where relevant logging exists.

File context includes NTFS metadata, Recycle Bin records, shortcuts and application-managed files.

No single artefact normally proves the whole event and correlation builds the stronger explanation.

A user profile may show which account context held an artefact but account context does not prove human identity.

The Registry may show what setting or system state was recorded but meaning varies by key and Windows version.

An event log may show what event a provider recorded but logging, retention and audit policy affect coverage.

An application artefact may show what an application recorded but the application defines creation and retention.

NTFS metadata may show where and when a file-system record changed but recorded times do not directly identify the user.

A Windows user profile groups a user's desktop, documents, application data and per-user Registry settings.

AppData\Local commonly stores device-specific data.

AppData\Roaming may follow a domain user between systems.

Profile paths can organise browser data, recent items, cloud-client files and application artefacts by account.

An artefact inside a profile links it to that profile context but does not by itself identify the human using the session.

Windows Registry system-wide hives include SYSTEM, SOFTWARE, SAM and SECURITY.

Per-user hives include NTUSER.DAT and UsrClass.dat.

A Registry value reflects stored configuration or recorded interaction and meaning depends on the key, Windows version and supporting transaction data.

Windows Event Logs include Security, System, Application and Operational channels.

Security logs contain logon and audit events where policy generated them.

System logs contain operating-system, driver and service events.

Application logs contain events written by applications and services.

Operational channels contain specialised records from Windows components.

Logs may wrap, be cleared or remain disabled and event presence and absence both require cautious interpretation.

The Master File Table can retain file names, allocation state, timestamps and storage references.

Recycle Bin records on supported Windows versions can retain original path, file size and deletion-related information.

The change journal may record file-system changes when enabled and still retained.

Deletion records support a file-system event but may not identify the person, reason or exact action that caused it.

A proposal in the Recycle Bin may support deletion from a profile context but not deliberate concealment.

LNK shortcut files may retain target paths, volume details and timestamps related to a referenced item.

Jump Lists automatic and custom destination files can record recent or pinned items for supported applications.

Shortcut and Jump List artefacts may show that a path or document appeared in a recent application context.

A reference does not prove that the file was read fully, copied or uploaded.

References may remain after the target file is unavailable.

Account context, application and event time should be corroborated before attribution.

Execution and activity artefacts include Prefetch, Amcache, SRUM, UserAssist and ShimCache.

Prefetch provides evidence related to application execution but availability and contents depend on Windows behaviour.

Amcache provides program and file inventory context but presence does not always prove execution.

SRUM provides application and network usage records but retention and interpretation depend on available tables.

UserAssist provides per-user graphical program activity but counts and timing require version-aware interpretation.

The presence and meaning of execution artefacts vary by Windows version, configuration and application behaviour.

Browser records include history, downloads, cookies and cached data.

Downloaded files can be examined through file metadata, browser databases and security-zone information.

Cloud-client artefacts include synchronisation folders, logs and configuration that may record local paths, account context and transfer activity.

Local records should be compared with server-side audit logs before attributing a share, upload or public link.

Windows examination in Autopsy involves Data Sources, File-system views, Extracted Results and Timeline.

Data Sources confirm the correct image, partitions, file system, image paths and ingest history.

File-system views examine user profiles, Windows directories, deleted files and relevant application folders.

Extracted Results review web, recent activity and other artefacts produced by enabled ingest modules.

Timeline filters events by source and artefact type and important records are verified in their original context.

Autopsy accelerates review but the examiner remains responsible for source verification and interpretation.

During acquisition the relevant storage must be preserved and every action, setting, error and verification result documented.

During examination work must be from a verified copy and each Windows artefact interpreted according to its origin and retention behaviour.

During reporting independent sources must be correlated, observation separated from inference and unresolved alternatives stated.

A reliable Windows conclusion depends on both the artefact and the process that preserved it.
